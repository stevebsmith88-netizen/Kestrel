2 new sets of numbers arrived late in 2026. On 18th November, [Job Bank refreshed its wages](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/adad580f-76b0-4502-bd05-20c125de9116) for opticians, optometric assistants, reception teams and practice managers. Around the same time, a survey of 460 optometrists gave a detailed look at what they earned in 2025, how they are paid and how much they work. Read side by side they say more than either does alone, and some of the figures surprised us.

## What optometrists reported

The survey is the [Canadian Optometry Landscape Report](LINK TO THE 2026 REPORT), written by Dr. Wynn Nguyen for Canadian New ODs and sponsored by OSI Group. It leans recent: 57% of the 460 respondents graduated in 2013 or later, and most trained at Waterloo. So it is a detailed picture of those respondents, not a count of every optometrist in the country. An [earlier edition](http://newods.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Canadian-New-ODs-FINAL-REPORT.pdf) ran in early 2025.

The median take-home income for 2025, before tax, was $175,000, from the 323 optometrists who answered and had worked a full year. Owners reported $200,000 and associates $168,310. Per day worked, the medians were $996 for owners and $775 for associates. By province, Ontario came in at about $169,000, Alberta at about $163,000 and British Columbia at $180,000. Saskatchewan showed $230,000, but from only 19 people, so treat it as a hint.

Smaller places paid more. Optometrists in communities under 10,000 reported a median of $190,000 and in cities of 10,000 to 100,000 $185,000, against $160,000 in cities over a million. Per day, it was $993 in the smallest places and $800 in the largest. Only 33 respondents were in the smallest group, so that is a direction and not a rule. If you run a clinic in a smaller town, it fits what owners there tell us about hiring (more on that below).

A typical week was shorter than many people assume: 4 days, about 33 hours with patients, 15 patients a day (12 of them full exams) and 24 days of vacation. Associates worked 4.5 days a week to owners' 4. The report also found that a longer week did not pay more. Median income peaked at $190,000 for people working 4.5 days and slipped to $175,000 for those working 5.5 days or more, while income per day fell from about $1,000 at 3.5 days or fewer to $712 at 5.5 or more.

Then there is how people are paid, which is the part we hear about most from clinics and candidates. 9 in 10 associates are paid a percentage of what they produce. The median is 23%, and about 3 in 4 of those on a single percentage sit between 20% and 25%. A guaranteed minimum is less common: 32% of associates have one, usually a daily amount, with a median of $600 a day. It depends heavily on where you are. 63% of respondents in British Columbia and 62% in Alberta have a guarantee, against 21% in Ontario. It also depends on when you graduated: 39% of 2021 to 2025 graduates have one, against 17% to 21% of those who graduated before 2016. One gap is worth knowing about: the report did not ask whether a percentage covers exams only or also glasses and contact lenses, which is the first thing we tell optometrists to ask. Our guide to [how optometrist pay is built](/blog/optometrist-pay-canada) goes through it.

On the hiring side, 1 in 5 owners surveyed said they are struggling to recruit an associate, and 71% of those named too few candidates in their region. The gap between regions was wide: 40% of Prairie owners reported trouble, against 11% in Ontario, although the Prairie group is small. Of the owners who did hire, 63% found someone within a year and 15% searched for more than 2 years. Owners who were struggling were asked what daily minimum they would pay: the most common answer was $601 to $750, above today's $600 median, from 22 owners. For a clinic in that position, our [empty chair cost](/blog/empty-optician-chair-cost) post puts a number on waiting.

Keeping people turned on money and on raises. 70% of owners with associates said they had never given a percentage raise, and 55% of associates had never asked for one. Of those who asked, 68% got one. Among the 20 associates who asked and were refused, 16 named a reason to leave. That is a small group, but it is a sharp reminder that a refused raise is often the start of a resignation. If you are the one asking, our post on [how to ask for a raise](/blog/ask-for-a-raise) has a script. If you are the clinic, [why people leave](/blog/why-people-leave-jobs) is worth 5 minutes.

## What Job Bank says now, and how it compares with 2025

Job Bank's 2026 file replaces the one released in November 2025, and the survey years behind it moved from 2023 and 2024 to 2024 and 2025. The comparison below is between those 2 files. It is not a count of raises, and Job Bank does not publish how many people each figure is based on, so a swing in 1 province can be sampling as much as pay. You can see every role and province in our [pay table](/resources#pay) and the [2026 summary](/resources/eye-care-pay-summary).

For opticians, the typical province barely moved: across the 8 provinces we cover, 3 medians rose, 4 fell and 1 stayed put, and the typical change was nil. The headline is Ontario, where the 2026 median of $29.00 an hour is 21% above last year's file, while British Columbia slipped 6% to $29.00. Our [optician pay guide](/blog/optician-pay-canada) has the full table. For optometric assistants, every province rose, with a typical increase of 6%: British Columbia rose 12% to $28.00, Manitoba 10% to $22.00 and Ontario 9% to $24.00. See our [guide to optometric assistant jobs](/blog/optometric-assistant-jobs-canada) for what the work involves. Reception and patient care teams rose in 6 of 8 provinces, typically by 3%. Practice managers, using Job Bank's closest group, rose in 5 of 8 provinces, with a typical rise of 9% and Ontario up 15%, from $31.00 to $35.75.

Optometrists did not change at all, because Job Bank's optometrist figures still come from the 2021 Census. They will stay that way until newer census data arrives. Job Bank shows a median of $78,000 for optometrists in Ontario, which is less than half of the survey's roughly $169,000. The 2 measure different things. Job Bank's figure covers every optometrist in the census, including part time and self employed practitioners, and the survey leans to full time, recent graduates. It is the reason we do not use Job Bank for optometrist pay anywhere on Kestrel, and why this survey matters so much.

## What surprised us

The Ontario optician jump is the one we keep returning to. A 21% rise in a year is large, Job Bank does not explain it, and a single new year of survey data can do that to a province. We would wait for next year's file before calling it a trend.

The gap between Job Bank and the survey for optometrists was bigger than we expected, and so was the Ontario guarantee figure. Ontario is the province with the strongest rules on posting pay, yet only 21% of respondents there have a guaranteed minimum, against about 60% in the west. A floor is the 1 feature newer optometrists look for first, so it would not be a surprise to see it spread.

Working more did not pay more. And mentorship, which clinics often lead with, ranked last for 43% of optometrists choosing a practice, behind autonomy (39% ranked it first), income (30%) and clinic culture (23%). Newer graduates lean to income: 45% of 2021 to 2025 graduates put it first, against 19% of those who graduated before 2000. That does not mean mentorship does not matter, only that few people pick a clinic for it. It tends to matter once they are there.

## What to watch in 2027

None of this is a forecast. These are the things we will be checking.

Posted pay. Since 1st January 2026, larger Ontario employers (the rules apply from about 25 employees) must state a pay range of no more than $50,000 in a public job ad, say whether the job is an existing vacancy, say whether they use AI to screen applicants, avoid asking for Canadian experience, and tell interviewed candidates within 45 days whether a decision has been made. The wording is in [O. Reg. 476/24](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/240476), and the province's own [guide to recent changes](https://www.ontario.ca/document/your-guide-employment-standards-act-0/recent-changes) explains it in plain terms. We expect more real pay ranges in job ads this year, and, once Kestrel has enough posted jobs, we plan to publish typical posted pay beside Job Bank, labelled as its own source. Our [job ad checklist](/blog/eye-care-job-ad-checklist) covers what a good ad includes.

Guarantees. If 39% of recent graduates having a guaranteed minimum is a sign of where offers are heading, clinics that do not post a floor will find it harder to compete. Ontario, at 21%, has the most room to move. See [independent or corporate](/blog/independent-or-corporate-eye-care) for how different kinds of clinic approach this.

The Prairies. If owners there keep struggling to recruit, we would expect to see more daily minimums, and a closer look at pay in smaller communities. The [Canadian Association of Optometrists](https://opto.ca/sites/default/files/2026-02/Optometry_in_Canada.2026.ENG_.pdf) tracks the workforce in its 2026 "Optometry in Canada" report, and our own [post on who works in eye care](/blog/who-works-in-eye-care-canada) uses census figures for the wider picture.

The next Job Bank file, due in November or December, will show whether Ontario's optician figure holds. Another edition of the survey next year would turn these comparisons into a real year on year series.

## If you are hiring, and if you are looking

If you are hiring, the 3 things the numbers point to are simple: say what a percentage is a percentage of, post a daily or annual floor, and describe the week (days, patient load, who covers Saturdays). Our post on [attracting the right candidates](/blog/attract-right-candidates) goes into each.

If you are looking, ask what the percentage covers, ask for the clinic's own estimate of what the role earns in dollars, and ask when the first pay review happens. If you are weighing a path into ownership, the survey found that about half of the optometrists who started in private practice or beside an independent optical are owners today, against 25% to 31% of those who started in corporate or franchise settings. See [how to become an optometrist](/blog/how-to-become-an-optometrist-canada) and [is optometry a good career](/blog/is-optometry-a-good-career-canada) for where that path starts.
